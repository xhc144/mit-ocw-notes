Strong law of large numbers


    I Suppose Xi are i.i.d. random variables with mean µ.

    I Then the value An := X1 +X2 +...+Xn is called the empirical
                                  n
      average of the ﬁrst n trials.
    I Intuition: when n is large, An is typically close to µ.

    I Recall: weak law of large numbers states that for all  > 0
      we have limn→∞ P{|An − µ| > } = 0.
    I The strong law of large numbers states that with
      probability one limn→∞ An = µ.
    I It is called “strong” because it implies the weak law of large
      numbers. But it takes a bit of thought to see why this is the
      case.

Strong law implies weak law


    I Suppose we know that the strong law holds, i.e., with
      probability 1 we have limn→∞ An = µ.
    I Strong law implies that for every  the random variable
      Y = max{n : |An − µ| > } is ﬁnite with probability one. It
      has some probability mass function (though we don’t know
      what it is).
    I Note that if |An − µ| >  for some n value then Y ≥ n.

    I Thus for each n we have P{|An − µ| > } ≤ P{Y ≥ n}.

    I So limn→∞ P{|An − µ| > } ≤ limn→∞ P{Y ≥ n} = 0.

    I If the right limit is zero for each  (strong law) then the left
      limit is zero for each  (weak law).
